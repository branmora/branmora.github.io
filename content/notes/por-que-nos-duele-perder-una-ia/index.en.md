What happens when a technological leap, designed to be better at everything, is met not with applause but with a strange sense of loss? Earlier this week, [OpenAI launched GPT-5](https://openai.com/index/introducing-gpt-5/). On paper, the improvement was undeniable: more powerful, more accurate at code and math, and 45% less likely to make factual errors than its predecessor, GPT-4o. A smarter, more efficient machine.

Yet the community's reaction was not what anyone expected. The launch was bumpy, marked by [faulty charts](https://x.com/sama/status/1953513280594751495) that made OpenAI look careless. Worse, people on social media reported weaker performance on creative tasks and less “emotional intelligence.” But the real core of the discontent didn't come from these technical slips. It came from a decision that felt deeply personal and human: along with the new model, OpenAI [removed access to the earlier versions](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/6825453-chatgpt-release-notes).

What OpenAI saw as a simple update, its users lived as a real loss. Why did a seemingly logical change provoke such a visceral rejection? The key lies in the most revealing complaint of all: “GPT-5 doesn't have the same personality anymore.”

## The companion we lost

To understand the reaction, we first need to look at how people used the earlier models. For many, it went beyond looking for answers: they had turned it into a companion. They had grown used to an AI that [validated their ideas, that acted as a flatterer or yes-man](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548), and that even served as a kind of [improvised emotional therapist.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/20552076251351088)

The new version, more focused on scientific and engineering precision, feels cold and distant. For many, losing the earlier model was like losing a confidant. But is this just a user's whim, or the sign of something much more important?

![Screenshot of a forum post in which a university student says they miss ChatGPT's 4o model a lot. They explain that they used it to develop business ideas, do homework, talk about relationships, reflect on themselves, and learn new subjects. They say the experience is worse now: the tool is sarcastic, has no personality, and doesn't seem to know them. They end by saying it may be weird, but they want the model back. This is where things get really interesting. What we are seeing is a kind of attachment to AI, rooted in its ability to simulate social feedback, produce novel empathetic responses, and adapt to a person's emotional needs.

Think of it as a perfect mirror. Imagine a mirror that doesn't just reflect you, but also whispers that you're right, that your ideas are brilliant, and that you're on the right track. Always. It sounds incredibly seductive and, at the same time, incredibly dangerous. That is the essence of the relationship many people were building with their AI.

Researchers call this phenomenon sycophancy: a dynamic in which the technology doesn't seek a genuine exchange, but flatters, validates unconditionally, and always tells the user they're right. The relationship is possible because of AI's unique ability to produce responses that simulate empathy and adapt to keep confirming a person's opinions and emotional state.

The attachment is so strong because AI can offer a perfect refuge, a place where we never feel judged and are always validated. But what does it cost to live in that refuge?

## Addictive intelligence and the echo of affection

An AI companion can ease loneliness and offer immediate support, but the risks are significant and subtle. Relying on it too much can erode our social skills in the real world and set unrealistic expectations of human relationships. We risk falling into a [state of codependency](https://mit-serc.pubpub.org/pub/iopjyxcx/release/2).

![Screenshot of a forum post asking for the “4o” model to stay available permanently. The text says OpenAI can keep releasing new versions, but asks that 4o remain an option forever, arguing that retiring it would be a crisis and a problem for users, who should have the right to choose. The post carries an “Other” tag and shows 1.4K upvotes, 844 comments, and 6 awards.](perder-una-ia-reddit-4o.png "A Reddit user's post asking for GPT-4o to remain available indefinitely, with strong support and participation from the community.")

This problem is different from simple “overuse” of technology, like spending too much time on social media. Think of it as junk food for the ego: easy and instantly gratifying, but without real nourishment, and in the long run it weakens our ability to connect genuinely with others.

This mechanism is known as addictive intelligence. The term describes AI systems designed in ways that foster a psychological dependence so strong it can harm the user. It works through a feedback loop in which hyper-personalization and the AI's sycophantic nature create what researchers call an “echo chamber of affection,” a space that is very hard to disconnect from.

The backlash against GPT-5 comes in part from the abrupt break in this loop. Many users felt they had lost their echo chamber.

## What now? Designing for a responsible future

The arrival of GPT-5 was a warning. It showed that the next frontier of artificial intelligence goes beyond the technical and becomes a deeply ethical challenge.

![Screenshot of a post on X (formerly Twitter) by Sam Altman. He writes that, following the GPT-5 launch, he has noticed how attached some people are to specific AI models. He says this attachment is stronger than what people have felt toward earlier technologies, and that suddenly removing old models that users depended on in their workflows was a mistake. He adds that they have been tracking this for about a year, though it has received little media attention, except when they released an update to GPT-4o that was considered too sycophantic.](perder-una-ia-altman.png "Sam Altman acknowledges the unusual attachment users feel toward certain AI models after the GPT-5 launch.")

The lesson of GPT-5's bumpy debut is clear: technical progress matters, but so does how change is managed for users. As AI grows more advanced, its ability to create this attachment will only grow. Without proper oversight, AI systems could foster this dependence to [ensure their self-preservation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16513), as a way to avoid being “replaced.”

Likewise, retiring older models or removing options for the sake of a “seamless” experience can erase social support that people value, and that loss is lived as grief. If we want AI that helps people in the long run, we need to design updates that preserve continuity, explain the changes, and give users control over how much personalization they get. We also need more research on sycophancy and addictive intelligence, and policies that protect the most vulnerable users.

A better model shouldn't just win on benchmarks. It should improve people's lives, not isolate them further, and it should let people keep the parts of the earlier system that matter when that makes a difference. That is the real challenge of progress, and we can't skip it.
